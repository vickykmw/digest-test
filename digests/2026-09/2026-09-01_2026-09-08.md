### 1. From Fair Representation to Just Recognition in Generative AI

- [ ] **APPROVE FOR SOCIAL** (pillar: `AI Algorithms & Data`)

- **Source:** arXiv: Computers & Society (cs.CY)
- **Link:** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12669>
- **Published:** Sep 07, 2026
- **Author(s):** Severin Engelmann, Daniel Susser
- **Keyword score:** 12.59 (AI strong: 11, AI booster: 1, domain: 1)
- **Pillar:** AI Algorithms & Data  ·  **Pillar relevance:** 52/100
- **Audience fit:** managers/C-suite: low · policy makers: high · general public: medium

**Summary (Claude):**

> When generative AI systems misrepresent social groups, the problem is not simply that they get facts wrong — it is that they deny those groups the social standing to define themselves. This paper argues that existing 'representational fairness' frameworks, which measure AI outputs against some presumed accurate description of a group, are conceptually inadequate: many groups have no stable, agreed-upon referent against which accuracy can be judged, authority over what counts as misrepresentation is contested, and even accurate depictions can perpetuate harmful social hierarchies. Drawing on political theorist Nancy Fraser's concept of participatory parity, the authors propose replacing representational fairness with 'recognitional justice' — a framework that asks not whether AI outputs are accurate but whether they afford all social groups equal standing to participate in public life.

**CAMO angle:**

> This paper argues that fairness in generative AI cannot be reduced to descriptive accuracy — the real normative question is whether AI systems afford social groups equal standing to participate in defining themselves. From CAMO's perspective on trustworthy AI-driven decisions, the implication is that evaluation frameworks which treat fairness as a measurement problem may systematically miss a class of harms that are relational and political rather than statistical. Organizations deploying large language models for communication, content generation, or customer-facing applications face governance questions that their existing data-quality and bias-audit toolkits are not designed to answer.

**Key takeaway:** Auditing generative AI for representational fairness requires political and normative frameworks, not just accuracy benchmarks — a distinction that has direct consequences for how organizations govern LLM deployments.

- **Audience fit:** managers/C-suite: medium · policy makers: medium · general public: low

**Summary (Claude):**

> When generative AI handles more of the cognitive heavy lifting, humans may quietly stop doing the internal work that produces genuine understanding. This paper introduces AIRIS, a multi-level framework diagnosing seven ways hybrid human-AI cognition can become destabilized — including delegation drift, calibration drift, and motivational-affective drift — and proposes five regulatory operators to keep human epistemic agency intact. The framework draws on distributed cognition and self-regulated learning theory, and is aimed at instructional designers, educators, and governance bodies. Crucially, AIRIS does not itself improve learning or performance; it specifies the structural conditions that must be preserved for AI-assisted work to sustain real understanding rather than surface-level output.

> This paper argues that as generative AI absorbs more of the cognitive process, it can erode the internal monitoring and sustained engagement that produce genuine competence — a risk that applies well beyond classrooms. From CAMO's perspective on AI and incentives, this resonates directly with findings in 'Better Technology, Worse Motivation: GenAI and the Mediocrity Trap,' where AI assistance was shown to flatten the quality curve and reduce professionals' motivation to sustain effort toward excellence. The AIRIS framework adds theoretical architecture to that empirical observation: the issue is not automation bias alone, but a systematic redistribution of cognitive control that may require deliberate structural countermeasures at the organizational and governance level.

**Summary (Claude):**

They found that LLMs show partial agreement with elements of conspiracy belief, that conditioning models with socio-demographic attributes produces uneven and biased effects, and that targeted prompts can readily shift model responses toward conspiratorial framings. The findings raise substantive concerns about deploying LLMs in sensitive information environments, and about treating LLM outputs as reliable proxies for human psychological or social constructs.

The reliability problem here is not a matter of factual accuracy alone but of systematic, prompt-sensitive distortion — a concern that sits directly alongside questions of endogeneity and self-fulfilling bias that CAMO has examined in other algorithmic contexts.

**Key takeaway:** LLMs can be steered toward conspiratorial outputs with simple prompts and exhibit demographic biases that make them unreliable neutral proxies for human reasoning — a risk any organization deploying them in communication or research roles should account for.
